Maybe it's the start of a new year that makes me want to shake things up a bit, and the first step is getting back into writing.

I’ve always felt that writing technical blogs can be a bit tricky: in most cases, what I write is just a restatement of other resources or a record of a problem and its solution. As an ordinary engineer, I don’t really need to elaborate too much on my personal views. Besides, technical articles are time-sensitive, so every few years my old posts end up getting discarded.

But in the past two years, I’ve noticed the ways some engineers use ChatGPT, and it made me reconsider what writing is for. Rather than focusing solely on the technical side, why not treat writing as a tool for capturing reflections that go beyond technology? After all, there’s background context an AI can’t get or truly understand—like why certain decisions are made, and the trade-offs behind them. Instead of just offering an answer, I’m more interested in the root of the problem and the ways of thinking that help people grow over the long term.

Writing can do more than solve issues; it can also capture how we feel in the moment. Our everyday experiences of the world are our most precious assets. These small, ordinary moments form the main thread of our lives, and by writing them down, we can reflect on ourselves, avoid forgetting, and hold on to the essence of our experiences.

Besides writing, I’ve started exploring a few new tools, such as Neovim, Tana, and Cursor AI.

I decided to try Neovim because seeing avante.nvim made me realize Emacs hasn’t really boosted my efficiency; more often than not, I’ve just been wrestling with upgrade issues. Since switching to Neovim, I’ve written roughly a third of this month’s code in it. Whenever I switch to another IDE, I find myself missing Neovim’s convenient key mappings.

Tana, on the other hand, was an unexpected delight. Initially, I worried that Supertags would lead me into a complex management process. But after a few days, I found it perfectly matched my way of thinking. Compared to Notion, Tana feels “quieter” and doesn’t interrupt my flow, while Supertags’ Fields and Commands offer a lot of convenience. I’ve never invested this much effort in a note-taking tool before, and I plan to explore more of its features in depth.

Lastly, there’s Cursor. Although I’ve always believed AI would fundamentally change how we program, until recently I only thought of GitHub Copilot as a more advanced auto-complete tool. It wasn’t until I tried windsurf and Cursor that I realized the paradigm shift has already arrived. I can’t say yet if I’ll come to love it, but I do know I want to be part of the journey.
